\paragraph{Result 3: Chain of Thought Amplifies the Mapping, It Does Not Build One.}

Results~1 and~2 measured the decision before the model writes anything. We now
let it reason. Probe~3 reads the answer the model commits to after generating
its own chain of thought, on the same steps and the same histories as Probe~2,
so the difference between them isolates the effect of reasoning.

\textbf{\textit{The gain from reasoning tracks the mapping the model already
had.}}
In the game theoretic scenarios, the three models with the strongest pre-CoT
mapping gain the most from reasoning: \model{Qwen-3.6} 27B improves by
\(18.8\) points, \model{Gemma-4} 31B by \(11.6\), and \model{Llama-3.3} 70B by
\(10.5\). The models with the weakest pre-CoT mapping gain nothing.
\model{Llama-3.2} 3B loses \(0.8\) points and \model{Gemma-2} 2B loses
\(9.0\). Across the seven non-reasoning models the improvement correlates with
pre-CoT agreement at \(r=0.72\). Reasoning helps where a mapping already
exists, and scale is not what sorts the models: \model{Qwen-3.5} 4B has the
second-largest improvement among terminated responses and one of the smallest
once its non-terminating steps are counted.

\textbf{\textit{Where the mapping is weak, reasoning does not correct, it
churns.}}
To see what reasoning actually does to a decision, we condition on the pre-CoT
answer. Among steps the model got wrong before reasoning, the \emph{salvage
rate} is the fraction that reasoning turns correct; among steps it got right,
the \emph{break rate} is the fraction it turns wrong. Where the mapping works,
salvage far exceeds break: \model{Gemma-4} 31B salvages \(73.0\%\) and breaks
\(2.0\%\), \model{Qwen-3.6} 27B salvages \(84.8\%\) and breaks \(5.8\%\).
Where it does not, the two rates converge. \model{Gemma-2} 2B salvages
\(59.3\%\) and breaks \(60.6\%\). Its reasoning moves decisions in both
directions at the same rate, which is why its accuracy falls rather than
rises. The ratio of salvage to break separates the two groups without overlap:
\(10\) to \(37\) for the three largest models, \(1.0\) to \(2.0\) for the four
smallest.

\textbf{\textit{When the mapping is absent, reasoning does not terminate.}}
The two reasoning distilled models fail in a different way. They are almost
never wrong and almost never decided. \model{R1-Qwen} 7B commits to an answer
on \(4.5\%\) of game theoretic steps and \model{R1-Llama} 70B on \(17.8\%\).
Termination is ordered by the same difficulty that orders the mapping: for
\model{R1-Llama} 70B it falls from \(97\%\) on \textit{Bandit} to \(43\%\) on
\textit{Trust Game} to \(13\%\) on \textit{Stag Hunt}. Scaling the distillation
from 7B to 70B restores commitment on the inference scenarios but not on the
game theoretic ones. Given a decision it cannot map, the model does not reason
its way to an answer; it relitigates one it has already reached.

\textbf{\textit{Reasoning and steering fail in different places, and neither
builds a mapping.}}
The two interventions are not interchangeable. Steering acts through the
model's own belief to action pathway, and in the game theoretic scenarios that
pathway carries almost nothing: the intervention rescues \(2.9\%\) of incorrect
decisions against \(2.0\%\) for matched random directions, for every model.
Reasoning is not subject to that limit, because it does not use the pathway at
all. It substitutes an external, serialized computation for the internal
mapping, and it succeeds in exactly the scenarios where steering fails,
provided the model can carry the computation out. That proviso is the
constraint. Reasoning is not a substitute a model can always run: the models
whose internal mapping is weakest are the ones whose reasoning churns or fails
to terminate. Neither intervention builds a mapping where none exists. One
amplifies a pathway that must already be there, and the other replaces it with
a computation the model must already be able to perform.
